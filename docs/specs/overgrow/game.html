A game, and a simulation of the food system

Overgrow

Grow food from two beds behind a house to the whole planet. Every level is a zoom-out: what you managed up close becomes one building block in the next map, the clock speeds up, and the same real-world mechanisms run underneath the whole way, from a compost heap to the climate.

Eight levels, one game

Each level's unit is the level below, sealed into one node. The garden becomes a plot; twelve plots are an allotment; the allotment becomes a field; and so on up to a country on the world map. The core problem changes at every scale, and so does how fast the clock runs.

How you play: you decide, people do

You never touch a bed, a field or a lorry. You set a plan, buy a tool, set a policy or, at the top, change a law; the gardener, the farmer, the hands, the drivers and eventually the whole country act on it, and you watch it happen on the map. Their time is the first constraint: a watering-can round takes the gardener a morning, a hose half of it, drip lines none.

Money buys tools, tools buy time, time buys yield. At the top a law buys or costs the same things for a whole country, with consequences you can trace back down.

People have goals, and later you don't choose them

Everyone you delegate to has skills you can see, goals you can only infer (money, an easy life, status, a belief in cheap food or the countryside) and an integrity you can't see at all. A good manager with the wrong goal is the wrong hire. From the smallholding up you hire; from the town up the people who matter most are chosen by others: the council by voters, the supermarket's buyer by the supermarket, the government by an election. Their goals can differ from yours and they can undo your work. The levers change from choose to persuade, ally, trade favours, wait for the election, or change what voters want. You are the constant across governments: the steward of the food system at each scale.

The model underneath: one graph at every scale

A bed, a herd, a shop, a country: to the engine they are all the same kind of thing, a node with stocks, flows and levers. That's what lets the planet be the same game as the garden, and lets the national carbon account be the sum of things you handled with a compost heap.

Twenty-five systems in five groups. Each implements a real mechanism (FAO-56 for water, degree days for growth, IPCC factors for gases, the principal–agent problem for people) with rough numbers tuned inside the size those sources give. The loops that make it a game

Steering the planet towards plant-based or towards meat is a real strategy with real trade-offs, felt at different speeds. These are the loops the player learns to see.

Zooming out, and back in

When you step up, the level you played is sealed into one node of the next map, keeping its totals over its last full cycle: output, quality, reliability, upkeep, health, carbon and land. A sealed node keeps running by those numbers alone; nothing inside it is simulated in detail. Events keep their effect and lose their detail: the expected food lost is the same whichever level shows it.

What ships first: the garden, the allotment and the smallholding

The owner chose three levels for launch: the Back Garden complete, the Allotment complete (a full year, sealed at the end) and the Smallholding's first year, built on the graph rather than as a garden-shaped special case: the node shape, flows in real units, sealing, carbon and land counted from the first bed, the Explain card, one adviser, the gardener who does the work, and the bot. Places are invented everywhere: a generated, UK-flavoured world from the seed, with real datasets behind the numbers and never as names.

The first minute

No menu and no scenario choice. The page opens on the garden early on a spring morning with the gardener standing by the shed. At 1× a game hour is a real second, so the first real minute is two and a half game days.

What makes the step up feel earned

The allotment committee offers a plot when your garden, sealed, would be a plot worth having: steady output, reliability and health over the last 28 days, shown on the goal bar so you can see that a steady, healthy garden is what's wanted rather than one lucky harvest. The step-up card shows the garden shrinking into a tile with its numbers on it, and the tile lands among eleven neighbours'. The old skills come straight back as new problems: one shared trough with a rota, surplus to swap, slugs from the neglected plot next door, a committee vote, and a neighbour who helps with your second plot and takes a little more than their share, which the map shows and the totals don't.

The first roadmap

Fifteen PR-sized parts: the graph and the clock; weather, soil and water; crops and the gardener; the bot and the first baselines; pests, wildlife and Explain; the shed and the advisers; sealing and the step up; the allotment's first season; the first zoom back in; the allotment's year; fields and the year plan; livestock; labour, machinery and energy; the first market and the road; the first release.

How balance is measured

A bot plays the same commands a player has, on seeds 1, 2 and 3, and reports the day of each milestone against agreed baselines. Strategy tests make sure no single trick wins: rotation beats one crop; "leave the pests" still gets there, later; handing everything to the advisers gets there within a quarter of the planning bot's time.

Real mechanisms, rough numbers

Every model names its sources and what it simplifies, and has a test asserting the direction and rough size of its effect: tomatoes in a hot dry week need more water than lettuce in cool rain; a day at 2 °C grows nothing. Constants are tuned inside the size the sources give.

How it's built

A static site on GitHub Pages: strict TypeScript built by Vite, Preact panels beside a WebGL map, and the simulation running in a Web Worker so a planet-sized graph never stalls the map on a phone. The same simulation module runs in Node for the tests and the bot.

Decided

The owner answered on 28 Sep: three levels ship at launch (through the Smallholding); one continuous save; the level names as above; no fail state that ends the game; invented places everywhere; the clock stops while the page is closed; £; the proposed allotment thresholds, tuned with the bot; the gardener's four hours a day. Every preference kept. And the art style: flat top-down and soft, picked from drawn examples of four. The spec is approved.
